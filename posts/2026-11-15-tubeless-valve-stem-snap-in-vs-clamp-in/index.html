Tubeless Valve Stems: Rubber Snap-In vs Metal Clamp-In

November 15, 2026 · By Zenyare Tire
𝕏 Share Facebook Copy Link

Who we are: Tire of Moto is run by ZENYARE (Qingdao Zhengya Rubber Industrial Co., Ltd.), a manufacturer of motorcycle tires and inner tubes. We're a manufacturer, not a neutral test lab, and we haven't tested any valves for this article. What follows comes from valve makers' and tire makers' published information, with sources at the end. There are no shopping links.

The short answer: the valve your wheel needs is decided first by the size of the valve hole in the rim and by your motorcycle maker's parts information, not by a preference for rubber or metal. Pressure and speed limits are set for each valve model, and they vary a lot within each type. Michelin recommends replacing a rubber tubeless valve at every tire change, and replacing the seal of a metal valve at every tire change.

What is a snap-in valve and what is a clamp-in valve?

A snap-in valve is a tubeless valve with a rubber body that is pulled through the rim's valve hole until the rubber seats on both sides of the rim. TR412 and TR413 are snap-in designations used in valve makers' catalogs.

A clamp-in valve is a metal valve, such as nickel-plated brass, that passes through the valve hole and is held by a nut on the outside of the rim, with a rubber seal between the valve and the rim.

Both types seal the air inside a tubeless tire. Neither is an inner-tube valve: on a tube-type wheel the valve is part of the tube, which is a different product.

How do I know which valve my rim takes?

Start with the valve hole diameter. Valve makers list each valve for a specific hole size, and the examples in one maker's motorcycle catalog show how much this varies:

Valve (Alligator catalog listing)TypeRim hole diameter
TR412, straightRubber snap-in11.3 + 0.4 mm
Motorcycle valve, 25 mm and TR430EQ 34 mmMetal clamp-in8.3 + 0.3 mm
90° motorcycle valves in the same catalogRubber snap-in8.8 mm or 11.3 mm

REMA TIP TOP lists its TR-412 and TR-413 snap-in valves for a rim hole of .453 inch, which is about 11.5 mm.

So a rim drilled for an 8.3 mm clamp-in valve and a rim drilled for an 11.3 mm snap-in valve are not interchangeable without the right valve. A valve made for a different hole size will not seat as its maker intends.

The practical steps:

  1. Check your owner's manual or the dealer parts catalog for the valve part number fitted at the factory.
  2. If you are replacing a valve that is already in the wheel, note whether it is rubber or metal, straight or angled, and its length.
  3. Measure the hole only with the old valve removed, and match it to the hole size the valve maker lists.

The catalog lists angled versions of both types. Michelin notes, in its inner tube guide, that when the braking system makes the valve hard to reach, an angled valve can be easier to inflate than a straight one.

Are rubber snap-in valves safe at highway speed?

It depends on the valve model, and the rating is printed by its maker. The same catalog shows how wide the range is:

ValveTypeMaker's pressure limitMaker's speed note
Alligator TR412 straightRubber snap-in4.5 bar"Speed km/h max: 210.0"
REMA TIP TOP TR-412, TR-413Rubber snap-in65 psiNot listed on the product page
90° motorcycle snap-in valves (same catalog)Rubber snap-inNot listed"Recommended maximum speed 80 km/h"
Alligator motorcycle clamp-in valvesMetal clamp-in14 barNot listed on the product page

What this table does and doesn't tell you:

  • A straight TR412 and a 90° snap-in from the same catalog carry very different speed notes (210 km/h and 80 km/h). "Rubber" alone tells you nothing about speed.
  • The metal clamp-in valves carry a much higher pressure limit than the TR412 snap-in (14 bar against 4.5 bar).
  • We did not find a published speed rating for the clamp-in valves above. That is not the same as "no limit". It means the product pages don't state one.

If your bike can exceed the speed a valve is rated for, that valve is the wrong choice for it. Check the rating of the exact valve you plan to fit, and use the valve type your motorcycle maker specifies.

When should I replace a tubeless valve?

Michelin's guidance on tubeless tires is direct:

  • "If you have a rubber valve: we recommend changing the valve at every tire change."
  • "If you have a metal valve: we recommend changing the valve seal at every tire change."

The valve catalog quoted above says that every time a tire is changed in the workshop, the valves "should be inspected and tested", whether they are rubber or metal. It names ozone, sunlight and chemicals among the things valves are exposed to in service.

For metal clamp-in valves, the same catalog adds a note for its motorcycle valves: "When replacing, always use a valve core with increased opening pressure." Ask the valve maker or your dealer which core matches the valve.

Why is my tubeless valve leaking?

A slow leak at the valve can come from more than one part of it. The valve catalog names defective rubber or metal valves and corrosion of the valve core among the causes of pressure loss. In practice, check these places separately:

  • The valve core, inside the stem.
  • The cap, which keeps dirt out of the core.
  • The seat or seal against the rim, where a snap-in body or a clamp-in seal meets the valve hole.

For fitting a snap-in TR412, the valve maker says the rim flange and the valve hole "must be thoroughly cleaned", the area around the hole should be checked for damage, and the valve should be coated with fitting lubricant before it is pulled in. A damaged hole or a valve meant for a different hole size is a reason the seat may not seal.

A leak that doesn't come from the valve can also come from the bead or the tire itself. If you've just fitted a tire and it won't hold air, our guide to a motorcycle tire bead that won't seat covers that side.

How tight should a clamp-in valve nut be?

Use the tightening torque in your motorcycle's service manual. We don't give one number here, because the right figure depends on your motorcycle and the valve fitted.

If you don't have the specification, ask your dealer to look it up before fitting the valve.

Can I switch from a rubber valve to a metal one?

Only if the metal valve is made for your rim's hole size and your motorcycle maker allows it. A clamp-in valve listed for an 8.3 mm hole is not a direct substitute for a snap-in listed for an 11.3 mm hole, and the reverse is also true. Check the rim, the valve maker's hole size and pressure and speed ratings, and your manual before changing type.

The same applies to tire sizes: only use a size approved by the motorcycle manufacturer or tire maker. Dunlop says a change should only be made "with the approval of the motorcycle or tire manufacturer." If you're choosing new tires at the same time, see how to choose motorcycle tires.

FAQ

Is a metal valve stem better than rubber on a motorcycle?

Not automatically. The valve makers we read rate each valve model for a hole size and a pressure, and some for a speed. In the catalog we read, the metal clamp-in valves are listed at 14 bar and the TR412 snap-in at 4.5 bar, but the right valve is the one that fits your rim's hole and matches your motorcycle maker's specification.

How often should I replace a tubeless valve stem?

Michelin recommends a new rubber valve at every tire change, and a new valve seal at every tire change for metal valves.

Can I reuse a snap-in valve when I change tires?

Michelin recommends changing a rubber valve at every tire change, so reusing one goes against that advice.

What does TR412 mean?

TR412 is a rubber snap-in tubeless valve designation. The Alligator catalog lists its TR412 under ETRTO number V2.03.6 for an 11.3 mm rim hole. REMA TIP TOP lists its TR-412 for a .453 inch hole, about 11.5 mm.
