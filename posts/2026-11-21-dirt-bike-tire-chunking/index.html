Dirt Bike Tire Chunking: Causes and How to Reduce It

November 21, 2026 · By Zenyare Tire
𝕏 Share Facebook Copy Link

Who we are: Tire of Moto is run by ZENYARE (Qingdao Zhengya Rubber Industrial Co., Ltd.), a manufacturer of motorcycle tires, inner tubes and mousse inserts. Nothing below promotes our products. What follows comes from what tire makers publish, with sources at the end.

"Chunking" is the rider's word for knobs tearing off a dirt bike tire, or pieces breaking off their edges, long before the tread is worn down. We looked for a tire maker that explains its causes directly and didn't find one. What tire makers do publish is how they design knobs to last, which tires suit which terrain, and how pressure and care affect wear.

So this guide is built from those sources. Where a cause is our reading of what the makers say, rather than their own words, we say so.

What causes dirt bike knobs to tear off?

No single cause is confirmed by a tire maker. Based on what they publish, these are the factors that may play a part:

The rest of this article goes through each one.

Does knob flex cause chunking?

It may be a factor. Dunlop doesn't use the word "chunking," but it describes design features aimed at knob durability and flex. On its Geomax MX33, "front and rear tires include tie bars that connect each knob, improving durability by reducing flex at the base of the knobs." On the MX54, Dunlop says a high-resilience compound "improves the rubber's strength, which increases traction and performance through less knob flex."

Our reading: if makers add tie bars and stronger compounds to cut flex at the base of the knob, that's likely a weak point. It doesn't tell you how much flex is too much, or that every tire without tie bars will chunk.

Can the wrong tire for the terrain cause chunking?

It may. Tire makers rate their off-road tires by surface. Dunlop calls the MX33 "an MX tire for soft-to-intermediate terrain" and the MX54 "an intermediate-to-hard terrain motocross tire." Its MX14 is rated for "extreme soft sand and muddy conditions." Bridgestone describes the Battlecross X40 as having "a wider utilisation range on hard terrain."

Tire makers don't say that a soft-terrain tire will chunk on hard ground. But if your tire is rated for sand or soft soil and you mostly ride hard-packed or rocky trails, it's worth checking whether a tire rated for that ground fits your bike. The maker's terrain rating is the place to start.

Does tire pressure affect chunking?

Michelin links pressure to tire damage and wear, though not to chunking by name. Its off-road advice says:

  • "Harder, rockier surfaces typically require slightly higher pressure to protect against rim and tire damage. Softer, sandy, or loamy surfaces allow lower pressure for more contact area and flotation."
  • "Tire pressure also has a direct impact on wear."
  • Going too low "risks pinch punctures when the tire is compressed against rocks or ruts, and rim damage."
  • "In tubeless setups, very low pressure can cause the bead to unseat from the rim."

Michelin's guidance for tubes is to stay "lower than road pressure but within a defined range, not as low as possible."

What pressure should I run on rocky terrain?

Use the figure your tire maker gives for that tire and that terrain. We don't give a general number, because tire makers don't. As an example of what a maker's table looks like, Michelin lists these cold pressures with an inner tube for some of its own tires:

Desert Race1.2 / 1.5 bar (17.4 / 21.8 psi)1.0 / 1.0 bar (14.5 / 14.5 psi)

These figures apply only to those Michelin tires, with an inner tube. Michelin says they depend on "the terrain, the weather conditions, the power of the bike and the type of control." For any other tire, ask its maker.

Michelin also says to check pressure cold, before each ride, and that "you should never deflate a tire that has just been ridden on."

Does heat cause tire chunking?

We didn't find a tire maker that says so. Bridgestone says the Battlecross X40's Modified Cooling Fin "draws cool air deep into the tyre tread to minimise heat build-up and ensure consistent performance." That tells you Bridgestone designs against heat build-up in the tread. It doesn't say heat causes knobs to tear, so we won't claim it does.

Can old or damaged tires chunk more easily?

It's possible, but no tire maker we checked says so directly about knobs. What they do say:

  • Michelin: after a session, "check the tire sidewall and tread for damage sustained from rocks or roots," and inspect the rim for impact damage.
  • Michelin: store tires away from sunlight, chemicals, solvents and heat, and away from anything sharp.
  • Dunlop: "Never use protectants, cleaners or dressings to enhance your tire appearance. These may degrade the rubber and remove the inherent ozone cracking/weather checking resistance."

A knob with a cut at its base, or rubber that's cracked from age or chemicals, may tear more easily. That's our inference, not a maker's statement. If you find cuts or cracks, ask the tire maker or a dealer whether the tire is still fit to use.

How to reduce dirt bike tire chunking

Steps 2 to 6 come from a tire maker's published advice; step 1 is our suggestion based on the makers' terrain ratings:

  1. Pick a tire rated for your usual terrain. Check the terrain the maker says the tire suits.
  2. Set pressure from the tire maker's figure for that tire and terrain, checked cold before each ride. Michelin says harder, rockier ground typically needs slightly higher pressure.
  3. Don't drop pressure as low as possible. Michelin warns of pinch flats, rim damage and, on tubeless setups, the bead unseating.
  4. Clean the tire after muddy rides and check tread and sidewall for rock and root damage (Michelin).
  5. Check knob height and wear uniformity regularly (Michelin).
  6. Skip tire shine products. Dunlop says they may degrade the rubber.

Michelin adds that its own off-road tires "may be reversed for extended use unless indicated by a directional arrow." That applies to Michelin tires; for any other brand, follow the arrow and the maker's instructions. Our guide to the rotation arrow explains what it means.

Do mousse inserts or rim locks help with chunking?

No tire maker we found says either one prevents chunking. They solve different problems. A mousse replaces the tube to prevent flats; Michelin gives its Bib Mousse a "pressure equivalence" of 0.8–1.0 bar (11.6–14.5 psi) and says it "can be used for a maximum of 6 months after fitting," with a shorter life in motocross than enduro. FAQ

Why are the knobs coming off my dirt bike tire?

Tire makers don't publish a single answer. From what they do publish, possible factors are knob flex at the base, a tire rated for different terrain, pressure that doesn't suit the surface, and cuts or degraded rubber.

Does low tire pressure cause chunking?

Michelin doesn't say that directly. It does say pressure has "a direct impact on wear," that harder, rockier surfaces typically need slightly higher pressure "to protect against rim and tire damage," and that going too low risks pinch flats and rim damage.

What tire pressure should I use for rocky terrain?

The figure your tire maker gives for that tire on hard or rocky ground. Michelin publishes a table for its own tires; it doesn't give one number for all tires.

Can I keep riding a tire with missing knobs?

We didn't find a tire maker rule on missing knobs. Michelin says knobs are "largely responsible for a tire's grip on the ground" and that grip drops as they wear. If knobs are missing or cut, ask the tire maker or a dealer.

Do tie bars stop chunking?

Dunlop says tie bars on the MX33 improve "durability by reducing flex at the base of the knobs." It doesn't promise they stop chunking.
